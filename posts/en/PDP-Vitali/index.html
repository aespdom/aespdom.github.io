On top of that, several months back I began to learn about totally distinct topic in 3-D algebraic topology, prompted by a former professor of mine. It was perhaps a bit over-ambitious to try to handle both projects at once given my other time constraints, but oh well, I guess I can’t help but learn these things the hard way still.

Anyhow, my thoughts on what to emphasize in this series and how to organize it have naturally changed in all this time. In truth, I had already snuck some of the changes in my thinking into the initial posts via numerous edits, some unannounced. In this post I want to introduce the periodic decomposition problem I have already referenced previously, state the theorem sitting at its foundation, and lay out the side of that theorem which has most strongly captured my imagination: its dependence on the Axiom of Choice. The fuller reframing of the series around this problem will come in the next post.

Actually, in the course of writing this post, I managed to figure out (with some help) how to resolve affirmatively one of the big questions on this set -theoretic side. Namely, the arguments I will present here prove that what I am calling the Periodic Decomposition Theorem (PDT) implies the existence of a Vitali set or Vitali selector, and that two natural restricted versions of the PDT are outright equivalent to the existence of such a selector. A Vitali selector is a set containing exactly one element from each equivalence class in \(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}\); as will be explained below, the existence of such sets is one of many weaker versions of the Axiom of Choice. In other words, the PDT, a foundational result in the literature on the periodic decomposition problem, is not a theorem of ZF set theory, and is at least as strong as a certain weak form of the Axiom of Choice. Whether the full PDT is equivalent to that weak form I will have to leave as a conjecture: that hinges on exactly how much choice the standard proof consumes, which I have not yet verified with the required care. I’ll explain all this in more detail below.

But first let me give some basic background on the periodic decomposition problem, and formally state the PDT. In the next section, I will be borrowing extensively from Farkas and Révész’s survey of the decomposition problem.1 Let’s jump in.

3 The Periodic Decomposition Problem

The periodic decomposition problem begins with the observation that if a function \(f: \mathbb{R}\to \mathbb{R}\) can be written as

\[f = f_1+\cdots +f_n, \tag{3.1}\]

where each \(f_i\) is periodic and has a period \(p_i\), then we must have

\[\Delta_{p_1}\cdots \Delta_{p_n} f = 0. \tag{3.2}\]

Thus, Equation 3.2 is a necessary condition for \(f\) to be decomposable in this manner. The decomposition problem then consists of determining when the implication can be reversed. This is a nice generalization of the fact that a function is periodic with period \(p\) if and only if \(\Delta_p f = 0\).

Notice that the identity \(\operatorname{Id}(x) = x\) always satisfies Equation 3.2 (for \(n\geq 2\)) no matter which \(p_i\) we choose. But if the \(p_i\) are all commensurable, the intersection \(\bigcap_i p_i\mathbb{Z}\) contains some nonzero number \(p\). This means it cannot be the case that \(\operatorname{Id}\) has a decomposition of the form Equation 3.1, for in that case the sum on the right-hand side of Equation 3.1 would be periodic with period \(p\), and \(\operatorname{Id}\) has no periods other than \(0\). However, for a system of \(n\) pairwise-incommensurable real numbers \(p_1,\ldots, p_n\), we do have the following theorem assuming the axiom of choice, which I will refer to as the Periodic Decomposition Theorem (PDT).

Theorem 3.1 (Periodic Decomposition Theorem (PDT)) Let \(p_1,\ldots, p_n\) be pairwise incommensurable. Then, for any function \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\), there exists a decomposition of the form Equation 3.1 for \(f\) if and only if Equation 3.2 holds.

This is a foundational result in the literature on the periodic decomposition problem. It appears to have been first proved by Mortola and Peirone in 1999.2 In the Farkas and Révész survey, it is stated as Corollary 6.3, which itself is a consequence of Theorem 5.9. The proof of this latter theorem is not in the survey however; for the proof one must see Theorem 2.3 in the 2008 paper of Farkas, Harangi, Keleti and Révész (FHKR).3

The proof of Theorem 2.3 in FHKR is very involved and beyond the scope of this post. I will say a little more about this later. But first, I want to write out the proof of the special case \(n=2\), since this proof will motivate our foray into world of fragments of the axiom of choice. From now on, I will refer to this special case as PDR\(_2\).

I learned the key idea (and the key application of the Axiom of Choice) for the proof of this case from the survey by Farkas and Révész (Proposition 1.3 there), though they leave the details for the reader to work out. Below is my attempt to do so; any errors are my own.

Before jumping in, it is worth recalling the definitions and notation used in Part II, in particular that \(P_f\) denotes the period module of a function \(f\), i.e. the group of all of its periods.

Proposition 3.1 (PDT\(_2\)) Let \(p, q\) be incommensurable. Then every function \(h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) satisfying \(\Delta_p\Delta_q h = 0\) can be written as \(h = f + g\) with \(p\in P_f\) and \(q\in P_g\).

Proof. Let \(G = p\mathbb{Z}+q\mathbb{Z}\), and consider the quotient \(\mathbb{R}/G\). For each coset in this quotient, choose a single representative (this is where we invoke AC); for any \(x\), let \(y_x\) denote its representative. Each \(x\) can be written uniquely as \(x = y_x + m_1p + m_2q\) for some integers \(m_1, m_2\). So, define the functions \[f:= x\mapsto h(y_x+ m_2q)\qquad\text{and}\qquad g: x\mapsto h(y_x+m_1p)-h(y_x).\] Since \(f(x)\) does not depend on \(m_1\) and \(g(x)\) does not depend on \(m_2\), we have \(p\in P_f\) and \(q\in P_g\).

It remains to check that \(h = f+g\), that is, that for each \(x\),

\[h(y_x + m_1p + m_2q) = h(y_x + m_2q) + h(y_x + m_1p) - h(y_x). \tag{3.3}\]

This follows from \(\Delta_p\Delta_q h = 0\) together with the expansion of a product of two difference operators,

\[\Delta_a\Delta_b h(t) = h(t+a+b) - h(t+b) - h(t+a) + h(t), \tag{3.4}\]

which holds for any \(t, a, b\in\mathbb{R}\). Here is where Farkas–Révész stop and leave the reader to fill in the gaps; so let’s fill them. Write \(|(m_1,m_2)| := |m_1| + |m_2|\), and note that \(\Delta_p\Delta_q h = 0\) if and only if \(\Delta_{\pm p}\Delta_{\pm q} h = 0\) for either choice of signs.4 We prove Equation 3.3 by induction on \(|(m_1,m_2)|\).

If \(m_1 = 0\), the second and third terms on the right-hand side of Equation 3.3 cancel and it reads \(h(y_x + m_2q) = h(y_x+m_2q)\); if \(m_2 = 0\), the first and third cancel. So Equation 3.3 holds outright whenever either coordinate vanishes, which covers the base case; assume from here on that both are nonzero.

Set \(a := \operatorname{sgn}(m_1)\,p\), \(b := \operatorname{sgn}(m_2)\,q\), and \(t := y_x + m_1'p + m_2'q\), where \(m_i' := m_i - \operatorname{sgn}(m_i)\). Since \(\Delta_a\Delta_b h = 0\), Equation 3.4 yields

\[h(y_x+m_1p+m_2q) = h(y_x+m_1'p+m_2q) + h(y_x+m_1p+m_2'q) - h(y_x+m_1'p+m_2'q).\]

Each \(m_i'\) is one step closer to zero than \(m_i\), so the three index pairs on the right all have norm strictly less than \(|(m_1,m_2)|\). Expanding each of them by the inductive hypothesis and simplifying gives Equation 3.3.

Notice how we defined \(f\) and \(g\) by grouping together (in one of two possible ways) terms in Equation 3.3 that share periods; \(x\mapsto h(y_x + m_2q)\) is \(p\)-periodic, \(x\mapsto h(y_x + m_1p)\) is \(q\)-periodic, and \(x\mapsto h(y_x)\) is both \(p\)- and \(q\)-periodic. We could have therefore defined \(f\) as \(f:x\mapsto h(y_x + m_2q) - h(y_x)\) and \(g\) as \(g:x\mapsto h(y_x + m_1p)\) instead, and we would have a perfectly valid decomposition. The point is grouping together terms that share periods.

Révész’s survey does not go into details of the proof for the case of arbitrary \(n\). Naturally I wondered if the technique would be basically the same. After thinking about it I came up with a very direct extension of the argument above, where now we divide by \(G = p_1\mathbb{Z}+\cdots+p_n\mathbb{Z}\), but only if we add the stipulation that the collection \(\{p_i\}\) is \(\mathbb{Q}\)-linearly independent (not just pairwise incommensurable). I will use PDT\(_*\) to refer to this weaker version of the PDT.

Proposition 3.2 (PDT\(_*\)) Let \(p_1,\ldots,p_n\) be \(\mathbb{Q}\)-linearly independent. Then every function \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) satisfying Equation 3.2 admits a decomposition of the form Equation 3.1.

If you followed the proof of PDT\(_2\) closely, you should have a good sense of how it generalizes. So as to not delay the main results of this post, I have moved the details to an appendix. The only thing we will need in what follows is which quotient the proof invokes AC for: exactly as in PDT\(_2\), it is \(\mathbb{R}/G\) with \(G = p_1\mathbb{Z}+\cdots+p_n\mathbb{Z}\), a countable subgroup which for \(n\geq 2\) has rank \(n\) and is therefore dense.

The \(\mathbb{Q}\)-linear independence condition is necessary for the proof of the PDT\(_*\) to work, because it requires uniquely writing each \(x\) as a sum \(x = y + \sum_i m_i p_i\) for some integers \(m_i\). The full proof of the PDT, where the \(p_i\) are only required to be pairwise incommensurable, is a lot more involved, and I will not go into it here. The interested reader can consult the FHKR 2008 paper for details; at some point I will probably write up a post about that paper as well once I have actually carefully read it myself. For our purposes, it suffices to know that the PDT is true in general under AC. Exactly how much choice its proof consumes is a more delicate question, and one I will return to below.

Let’s review now some of this set-theoretic terminology, and work out how much choice the proofs above actually require.

4 A little set theory

For the last 100 years, set theory has acted as the de facto foundation for most of mathematics. The majority of mathematicians use set theory fairly informally. They know set theorists have worked out all kinds of axiomatic systems to justify their manipulations with sets, and that the system called “Zermelo–Fraenkel Set Theory with the Axiom of Choice (ZFC)” in particular seems to work well for most of their purposes. But they often don’t concern themselves too much with the details of these axioms. In these posts, we will still use set theory relatively informally, but we will concern ourselves heavily with the exact dependence of statements on the Axiom of Choice.

Recall that the Axiom of Choice (AC) states that given any collection of nonempty sets \(\{S_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in \mathcal{I}}\), regardless of the cardinality of this collection, there exists a set consisting of exactly one element from each \(S_\alpha\). AC is equivalent to a great many other statements, including the Well-Ordering Theorem (every set can be well-ordered), and has weaker versions asserting the ability to choose one element from a more limited class of collections of sets. The most important weaker fragment of AC we care about here is the assertion that there exists a Vitali selector (call this assertion VIT for brevity); recall this is a set containing exactly one element from each equivalence class in \(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}\). Vitali sets are the quintessential (non-constructive) “examples” of nonmeasurable sets.

When I call this a “weaker” fragment, it is because AC of course implies VIT, but the other way around is not true. I.e., if we assume only the axioms of Zermelo–Fraenkel (ZF) set theory without assuming AC, it cannot be proved that AC is implied by VIT. In fact, the existence of a Vitali selector is weaker than another fragment of AC we will care about in this series: the existence of a Hamel basis, meaning a basis for \(\mathbb{R}\) as a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-vector space. In turn, the existence of Hamel bases is weaker than the axiom of choice for arbitrary collections of subsets of the real line (equivalently, the existence of a well-ordering of \(\mathbb{R}\)), and thus of course weaker than the full AC.5

However, VIT is still stronger than what can be proved in ZF alone. The famous witness to this is the Solovay model, a model of ZF (plus dependent choice) in which all subsets of the real line are Lebesgue measurable, so that no Vitali set can exist in it.6 Solovay’s construction assumes the consistency of an inaccessible cardinal, and a theorem of Shelah says that for full Lebesgue measurability that assumption cannot be dropped.

For our purposes, though, we need considerably less. We do not need every set to be measurable; we only need a model of ZF with no Vitali selector in it at all. And Shelah also produced a model of ZF + DC in which every set of reals has the Baire property, this time with no large-cardinal assumption whatsoever.7 Since a Vitali selector can never have the Baire property, such a model contains none, and so VIT — and with it everything we will derive from it below — is independent of ZF on nothing more than the consistency of ZF itself. In other words, VIT is genuinely stronger than ZF, and yet much, much weaker than the full Axiom of Choice.

As remarked above, the PDT and thus the weaker variants are theorems of ZFC. But we don’t really need the full AC for the arguments given so far. All these arguments need is the existence of a Vitali selector.

The reader might now wonder: wait, but we did not use a selector for \(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}\) in the proofs of the PDT\(_*\) and PDT\(_2\). Instead, Proposition 3.1 uses quotients with rank 2 groups, while Proposition 3.2 uses groups of finite rank \(\geq 2\). Moreover, I suspect, after a preliminary reading of the FHKR paper, that their proof of Theorem 3.1 in full generality only ever requires quotienting by rank 2 subgroups of \(\mathbb{R}\) as well.8 That last point is exactly the one I have not checked carefully enough, and I will come back to it further below, once we have the transfer theorem in hand. The question naturally arises: over ZF, what does the existence of any one of these selectors imply about the existence of one for the others and for \(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}\)?

5 Transferability of selectors and Borel equivalence relations

Well, it turns out a Vitali selector exists if and only if a selector for any one of these quotients exists. In fact, a stronger result holds, which I will call the selector transfer theorem; this theorem itself is the consequence of many even stronger and deeper theorems in descriptive set theory and the theory of Borel equivalence relations. This is the theorem:

Theorem 5.1 (Selector transfer) Let \(G\) be a countable, dense subgroup of \(\mathbb{R}\). Then a selector for \(\mathbb{R}/G\) exists if and only if a selector for \(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}\) exists.

I have not seen this result stated in quite this form anywhere in the literature; it is a synthesis of results drawn from several papers and preprints, some of them quite recent. Writing that synthesis out carefully turned into a post of its own, <em>Selectors on quotients of <span class="math inline">\(\mathbb{R}\)</span></em>, where I give the definitions it rests on and trace the route through the literature. Here I will simply take Theorem 5.1 as given, and note that we are leaning on some deep facts in descriptive set theory that belong to active areas of research.

With that in hand, we get an immediate corollary: Proposition 3.1 and Proposition 3.2 are theorems of ZF + VIT.

6 The PDT and the existence of a Vitali selector

Now we can tackle the main results of this post: over ZF, the PDT implies the existence of a Vitali selector, and the two restricted versions proved earlier are equivalent to it (and thus to each other).

First, let me explain the thought process that eventually led me here. Before I knew whether any of this was true, it was evident that the PDT does imply the existence of nonmeasurable (though not necessarily Vitali) sets, and is thus independent of ZF, per Solovay. This follows because \(\operatorname{Id}\) (and in fact any \(f\) with \(\lim_{x\to \infty} f(x) = \infty\)) cannot have a measurable decomposition (see the survey by Farkas and Révész),9 yet must have a decomposition in ZF + PDT. This suggested to me that a possible way to prove equivalence of the PDT to Vitali selectors is to show that a decomposition for \(\operatorname{Id}\) must induce a Vitali-type selector. I thought that maybe one of the nonmeasurable pre-images of a summand in the decomposition must actually be such a Vitali-type set.

But I was stuck on how to prove this, and I was not sure if it was even true. It turns out, as was pointed out to me, that I was halfway there. The function \(\operatorname{Id}\) is in fact the correct one to consider, but the Vitali set is not a pre-image of a summand in the decomposition. The key instead is to see that if \(x = f(x) + g(x)\) is a \((p,q)\)-decomposition of \(\operatorname{Id}\), then \(f(x + nq) = f(x) + nq\) and \(g(x+mp) = g(x) + mp\), and thus \(f(x)\in [0, q)\) and \(g(x)\in [0, p)\) can occur only once per coset. To put this more formally in a theorem, we get:

Theorem 6.1 Suppose \(p, q > 0\) with \(p/q\not\in \mathbb{Q}\), and suppose \(\operatorname{Id}\) admits a \((p, q)\)-decomposition, \(\operatorname{Id} = f + g\) with \(p\in P_f\) and \(q\in P_g\). Then the set \[S := \{x\in \mathbb{R}: \lfloor f(x)/q \rfloor = 0 = \lfloor g(x)/p \rfloor \} \tag{6.1}\] is a selector for \(\mathbb{R}/(p\mathbb{Z} + q\mathbb{Z})\).

Proof. Since \(\operatorname{Id} = f+g\) and \(nq\in P_g\) for every \(n\in\mathbb{Z}\),

\[f(x+nq) = (x+nq) - g(x+nq) = (x+nq) - g(x) = f(x) + nq,\]

and symmetrically \(g(x+mp) = g(x)+mp\) for every \(m\in\mathbb{Z}\), using \(mp\in P_f\).

Let \(x\in \mathbb{R}\). Then \(f(x) = nq + t\) for some \(n\in \mathbb{Z}\) and \(t\in [0, q)\), where \(n\) may be zero. This means \(x - nq\) is in the same coset as \(x\), and \(f(x-nq) = f(x) - nq = t\in [0, q)\). Similarly, \(g(x) = mp + s\) for some \(m\in \mathbb{Z}\) and \(s\in [0, p)\), and thus \(x - mp\) is in the same coset as \(x\), and \(g(x-mp) = g(x) - mp = s\in [0, p)\). Let \(y = x - nq - mp\). Then \(y\) is in the same coset as \(x\), and, since \(p\in P_f\) and \(q\in P_g\), we have \(f(y) = f(x-nq) = t\in [0, q)\) and \(g(y) = g(x-mp) = s\in [0, p)\). Thus \(y\in S\). In other words, each coset intersects \(S\) at least once.

Now we observe that this \(y\) must be unique. Let \(y_1, y_2\in S\) be in the same coset. Then \(y_1 - y_2 = mp + nq\) for some \(m, n\in \mathbb{Z}\). But then \(f(y_1) - f(y_2) = nq\) and \(g(y_1) - g(y_2) = mp\). Since \(f(y_i)\in [0, q)\) and \(g(y_i)\in [0, p)\), we must have \(n = m = 0\), and thus \(y_1 = y_2\). Therefore, each coset intersects \(S\) at most once. This finishes the proof that \(S\) is a selector for \(\mathbb{R}/(p\mathbb{Z} + q\mathbb{Z})\).

A perfectly simple, elementary proof that eluded me for months! Combining it with the two propositions proved earlier, we arrive at the main result we have been aiming for:

Corollary 6.1 Over ZF, the following three statements are equivalent:

  1. PDT\(_2\), i.e. Proposition 3.1;
  2. PDT\(_*\), i.e. Proposition 3.2, the version of the PDT restricted to \(\mathbb{Q}\)-linearly independent periods;
  3. there exists a Vitali selector.

Proof.

  1. \(\Rightarrow\) (2). The proof of Proposition 3.2 needs exactly one thing beyond ZF: a selector for \(\mathbb{R}/(p_1\mathbb{Z}+\cdots+p_n\mathbb{Z})\). That subgroup is countable, and since it has rank \(n\geq 2\) it is dense, so Theorem 5.1 manufactures such a selector from a Vitali selector.

  2. \(\Rightarrow\) (1). Immediate: for \(n = 2\), \(\mathbb{Q}\)-linear independence of \(\{p, q\}\) and incommensurability of \(p\) and \(q\) are the same condition.

  3. \(\Rightarrow\) (3). Fix positive incommensurable \(p, q\). Since \(\Delta_q\operatorname{Id}\) is the constant \(q\), we have \(\Delta_p\Delta_q \operatorname{Id} = 0\), so PDT\(_2\) hands us a \((p,q)\)-decomposition of \(\operatorname{Id}\). By Theorem 6.1 this yields a selector for \(\mathbb{R}/(p\mathbb{Z}+q\mathbb{Z})\), and by Theorem 5.1 a Vitali selector.

The full PDT contains PDT\(_2\) as the special case \(n = 2\), so the hard direction comes for free in general.

Corollary 6.2 Over ZF, the PDT implies the existence of a Vitali selector. In particular, the PDT is not a theorem of ZF.

It is worth emphasizing that this direction is unconditional. It rests only on Theorem 6.1 and Theorem 5.1, and does not depend on any reading of how FHKR prove the general case.

The converse is where I have to stop short of a proof.

Conjecture. Over ZF, the existence of a Vitali selector implies the PDT. Consequently, the PDT is equivalent to the existence of a Vitali selector.

What is missing is a careful accounting of the choice consumed by the FHKR proof of the general case. My preliminary reading, sketched in an earlier footnote, suggests that the only essential appeal to choice there is at the level of selectors for \(\mathbb{R}/(a_i\mathbb{Z}+a_j\mathbb{Z})\) with \(a_i/a_j\notin\mathbb{Q}\) — which is to say, at the level of a Vitali selector. But there is at least one step in that argument I have not checked with the care it deserves, and until I do, the conjecture stays a conjecture. Settling it is high on my list for a future post.

To me, this is pretty neat. But it is only the beginning. There are many possible variations of the PDT that can be considered by altering the conditions on the summands in the decomposition. For instance, we could require that the summands in a decomposition be not only periodic, but incommensurable (they won’t necessarily be incommensurable if the function being decomposed is already periodic, as we will see in the next post), or require that they be strictly periodic. We could also generalize from periodicity by considering operators \(T:A\to A\) on arbitrary sets and \(T\)-invariance of functions \(f:A\to \mathbb{R}\) defined by \(f(Tx) = f(x)\); this is actually the route taken in FHKR to prove the PDT as a corollary of a more general statement. In each such variation or generalization, we can ask what choice principles the resulting decomposition theorems require or are equivalent to. In fact, so far the variations I have considered seem to each require distinct levels of choice, and I suspect these distinct levels of choice I have used in the proofs are in fact equivalent to the theorems. I’ll be writing more about this in the future.

Conclusion

Here is why we should care about all this: AC is very powerful when it comes to proving that things exist. Indeed, in some ways, it is a little too powerful: AC together with the rest of ZF can be used to assert the existence of objects that are impossible to explicitly construct, and some of these are very bizarre (as Pathological Periodicity will attest to). This seems to be one of many factors that push a minority of mathematicians to reject ZFC as an axiom system.

I don’t think it is necessary to reject ZFC outright. I do think however that figuring out precisely how much various results throughout “standard” mathematics depend on AC and its weaker fragments is in and of itself highly interesting and worthwhile mathematics. Doing so gives us a more accurate picture of the spectrum of deductive strength — of exactly how much choice — underlying results throughout the parts of our discipline built over ZF, and hence of how far each of them sits from anything you could actually construct.

Philosophically, I think this matters because, even if you accept the existence of objects that cannot be constructed, such objects come with sharply limited domains of applicability. Grant a Hamel basis, for instance, and there exist additive functions \(\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) that fail to be linear; these are nowhere continuous, and their graphs are dense in the plane. But you will never write one down, never evaluate one at a point, never compute anything with one. Their existence is a fact about what ZFC proves, not a fact you can put to work to model the motion of ball or even the behavior of a quantum system. Locating a theorem on the spectrum above means knowing, for that theorem, roughly where this line falls.

A good chunk of Pathological Periodicity will be about parsing out this spectrum specifically within the literature on the Periodic Decomposition Problem. Today, we got a nice start doing so for the PDT and the weakened versions PDT\(_2\) and PDT\(_*\) discussed above.

Appendix: proof of PDT\(_*\)

Here is the proof of Proposition 3.2, deferred from earlier. Throughout, \(p_1,\ldots,p_n\) are \(\mathbb{Q}\)-linearly independent real numbers, \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) is a function satisfying Equation 3.2, and \(G := p_1\mathbb{Z}+\cdots+p_n\mathbb{Z}\).

Proof. The key is to expand out \(\Delta_{p_1}\cdots \Delta_{p_n} f\) in terms of translates of \(f\) by sums of the \(p_i\). Writing \(T_p f(x) := f(x+p)\) and letting \(I\) denote the identity operator, we have \(\Delta_p f = (T_p - I)f\), so Equation 3.2 becomes

\[\Delta_{p_1}\cdots \Delta_{p_n} f = (T_{p_1} - I)\cdots (T_{p_n} - I)f = 0. \tag{6.2}\]

Expanding out the product and writing \(p_S := \sum_{i\in S} p_i\) for any \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\), this says

\[\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}} (-1)^{n-|S|}\, T_{p_S} f = 0. \tag{6.3}\]

Note \(S\) can be the empty set, in which case \(p_\emptyset = 0\) and \(T_{p_\emptyset} = I\). Isolating the single term in which \(S\) is all of \(\{1,\ldots,n\}\), Equation 6.3 says that for every \(t\in\mathbb{R}\),

\[f(t + p_1+\cdots+p_n) = \sum_{S\subsetneq\{1,\ldots,n\}} (-1)^{n-|S|+1}\, f(t+p_S). \tag{6.4}\]

Using the Axiom of Choice (or just VIT), we fix a selector for \(\mathbb{R}/G\), and let \(y_x\) be the representative of \(x\). By the \(\mathbb{Q}\)-linear independence of the \(p_i\), we can write any \(x\) uniquely as \(x = y_x + \sum_i m_i p_i\) with \(m_i\in\mathbb{Z}\). Substituting \(t = y_x + \sum_i (m_i-1) p_i\) into Equation 6.4 gives a recursion on the multi-index \((m_1,\ldots,m_n)\), exactly as in the proof of PDT\(_2\) (every term on the right-hand side has at least one coordinate lowered from \(m_i\) to \(m_i - 1\)). Inducting on \(|m_1|,\ldots,|m_n|\) then yields the closed form

\[f(x) = \sum_{S\subsetneq\{1,\ldots,n\}} (-1)^{n-|S|+1}\, f\Big(y_x + \sum_{i\in S} m_i p_i\Big), \tag{6.5}\]

which for \(n=2\) is precisely Equation 3.3.

Now, consider the functions \(f_S := x\mapsto f\big(y_x + \sum_{i\in S} m_i p_i\big)\). For any \(j\notin S\), the value \(f_S(x)\) does not depend on the coordinate \(m_j\), and translating \(x\) by \(p_j\) changes \(m_j\) and nothing else; so \(f_S\) is \(p_j\)-periodic for every \(j\notin S\). Since \(S\) is a proper subset, there is always at least one such \(j\).

We define the \(f_i\) by once again grouping together terms that share periods. Like before, there are multiple ways to do so. For simplicity, and to match the choice made in the proof of PDT\(_2\), we assign each \(S\) to the largest index it omits:

\[f_j := \sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}_j} (-1)^{n-|S|+1}\, f_S, \qquad\text{where}\quad \mathcal{S}_j := \big\{\, S\subsetneq\{1,\ldots,n\} \;:\; j = \max(\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus S) \,\big\}.\]

Every proper subset omits at least one index and therefore has a largest omitted one, so each term of Equation 6.5 gets used exactly once and \(f = f_1+\cdots+f_n\). And each \(f_j\) is a sum of functions that are all \(p_j\)-periodic, so \(p_j \in P_{f_j}\). This is a decomposition of the form Equation 3.1.

The reader can verify that for \(n=2\) this recipe returns exactly the \(f\) and \(g\) of the proof of PDT\(_2\): the subsets \(\{1\}\) and \(\emptyset\) both have largest omitted index \(2\) and get collected into \(f_2 = g\), while \(\{2\}\) has largest omitted index \(1\) and gives \(f_1 = f\).
