Introduction

7 Recasting the series

To conclude, let’s recast Pathological Periodicity firmly as an exploration of certain aspects of the periodic decomposition problem.

First, observe that if a non-periodic function admits a periodic decomposition as in ?@eq-decomp, the summands must satisfy \(\bigcap_i P_{f_i} = \{0\}\). For \(n = 2\) this says precisely that \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) are incommensurable. This need not be the case if \(f\) is periodic; as we will see in a future post, the set of representatives of, say, \(\mathbb{R}/(p\mathbb{Z} + q\mathbb{Z})\) may induce common shared periods amongst the \(f_i\). The decompositions of periodic functions that are interesting, however, are those where the \(f_i\) are incommensurable with each other and with \(f\) itself.

Essentially, this series is an investigation into periodically decomposable functions, with special attention to decomposable functions that are themselves already periodic.

It is fairly obvious why one might be interested in periodic decompositions of non-periodic functions. But why care about periodic decomposability of functions that are already periodic?

Because studying this means studying the limits of periodicity’s closure with respect to the operation of addition. The set of periodic functions \(\mathscr{P}\) is closed under composition, but not under addition (or multiplication). We could leave things at that, but anyone with a dose of curiosity would want to understand more fully the extent to which additive (and multiplicative) algebraic structures exist or fail to exist within \(\mathscr{P}\). The fact that the boundaries of such algebraic structures are traced by analytic conditions (boundedness, continuity, measurability), as well as by the strength of choice principles adopted at the axiomatic level, is what makes this topic so interesting.

We can restate the results of Part II as follows.

Part II, Theorem 2.3 (restated). Suppose \(f\) is a periodic function that is bounded on some interval, and suppose \(q\) is a number such that \(\mathcal{C}(\Delta_q f)\) is nonempty. Then \(f\) does not have an incommensurable \((p, q)\)-decomposition for any \(p\).

Corollary. Suppose \(f\) is as above and \(\mathcal{C}(\Delta_q f)\) is nonempty for all \(q\). Then \(f\) is not 2-decomposable.

Part II, Theorem 2.2 (restated). Suppose \(f\) is a periodic function such that \(\mathcal{C}(f)\) is somewhere dense. Then \(f\) is not 2-decomposable. In particular, no continuous periodic function is 2-decomposable (Theorem 2.1 of Part II).

8 Degeneracy in \(n\)-ary decompositions

9 Terminology

Let’s formalize some terminology. I will say a function \(f\) admits a \((p_1, \ldots, p_n)\)-decomposition if it admits a decomposition as in ?@eq-decomp with \(f_i\) having period \(p_i\), and with the \(p_i\) being mutually incommensurable. This decomposition will be incommensurable if additionally the functions \(f_i\) are pairwise incommensurable. This decomposition will be strict if the \(f_i\) are strictly periodic with fundamental periods \(p_i\). Moreover, let’s agree to say \(f\) is \(n\)-decomposable if it admits an incommensurable \((p_1, \ldots, p_n)\)-decomposition, and to say it is decomposable if it is \(n\)-decomposable for some \(n\geq 2\).
